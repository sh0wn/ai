# No-Slop Writing

Readers spot AI-generated writing fast, and the tells are specific: certain words, a compulsive urge to bullet everything, throat-clearing before the point, compliments nobody asked for, and paragraphs that say one sentence's worth of content in five. None of that is about the ideas being wrong — it's packaging that undermines otherwise good content.

This is a revision pass. Draft the document normally with whatever skill applies (docx, pdf, pptx, xlsx, markdown), then run it through the eight checks below before it goes out. Reread the actual draft against each one — don't just skim.

## 1. Cut AI-isms and buzzwords

These are the single fastest way to make writing sound synthetic, because they're disproportionately common in AI output and rare in how people actually write. Delete or replace on sight.

| Cut | Because |
|---|---|
| delve into, dive into | nobody says this out loud — use "look at," "cover," or just start |
| tapestry, landscape, realm | vague scene-setting that says nothing concrete |
| testament to, a reflection of | inflates a simple cause into a grand statement |
| robust, seamless, holistic | filler adjectives — name the actual property instead |
| leverage (as a verb) | just say "use" |
| unlock, unleash, elevate, supercharge | marketing-speak for ordinary improvements |
| game-changer, cutting-edge, best-in-class, revolutionary, bespoke, dynamic | unearned superlatives — show the number instead of the adjective |
| foster, enhance, navigate, unpack | vague gestures at a contribution — name the specific action instead |
| beacon, synergy | grandiose abstractions that don't point to anything concrete |
| "it's not just X, it's Y" / "it's not about X; it's about Y" | a rhetorical crutch — say what it is, once |
| in today's fast-paced world / in an ever-evolving landscape / in today's digital landscape | throat-clearing before the actual sentence |
| moreover, furthermore, additionally (stacked repeatedly) | fine occasionally, but stacking them is a tic — use one or just start the next sentence |

If a sentence only exists to contain one of these phrases, the sentence usually isn't needed either.

## 2. Format only where it earns its place

Headers, bullets, and bold are for structure the reader actually needs — parallel items, steps in sequence, a table of options. They are not a default writing mode.

- Don't add a header for every paragraph. If a section is three sentences, it doesn't need its own heading.
- Don't convert a paragraph of connected reasoning into a bullet list. If idea B follows from idea A, that's prose, not two bullets.
- Don't bold every key term. Bold marks the one or two things a skimming reader must not miss — not a decoration applied evenly throughout.
- A document with a clear narrative arc (a memo making a case, a summary of findings) usually needs fewer headers than instinct suggests. Let the writing carry it.

## 3. Delete hedging, filler, and throat-clearing

- No "It's important to note that," "It's worth noting," "It's worth mentioning," "Needless to say" — if it's worth saying, say it; if it's needless, cut it.
- No warm-up sentences before the actual point ("When it comes to X, there are many factors to consider..."). Open with the point.
- No restating the request back to the reader ("You asked for a summary of Q3 performance, so here is..."). They know what they asked for — start with the answer.
- No closing summary that just repeats the document ("In conclusion, we've seen that..."). If the document made its case, it doesn't need to re-make it in miniature at the end. A closing section earns its place only when it adds something new — a decision needed, a next step, a risk to watch.
- No sweeping, aspirational sign-off either — "shaping the future," "driving innovation forward," and similar. End on the last concrete fact, decision, or next step, not a gesture at the horizon.

## 4. Remove sycophancy

A document isn't a conversation — there's no reader in the room to compliment. Cut anything praising the request, the topic, or the reader ("Great question," "This is an exciting area," "You're right to focus on this"). Also cut unearned enthusiasm about the subject matter itself — a status update doesn't need to sound thrilled about the status.

## 5. Cut padding — every sentence should do work

- If a paragraph can lose a sentence without losing meaning, cut the sentence.
- If a sentence says the same thing as the one before it in different words, cut one of them.
- Don't explain a term the intended reader already knows. Match the document's actual audience, not a generic one.
- Prefer the specific claim over the general one. "Response time improved" is padding; "response time dropped from 800ms to 220ms" is the sentence that was worth writing.
- Length should follow from content, not from a sense that a "real" document needs to be a certain size. A one-page memo that says everything it needs to is better than a three-page memo that says the same thing slower.

## 6. Avoid mechanical sentence patterns

These show up so often in AI output that readers clock them almost as fast as the vocabulary.

- Don't use the em dash to join clauses or land a dramatic pause. Use a period, a comma, or parentheses instead.
- Vary sentence length within a paragraph. A run of evenly-paced medium sentences reads mechanically — mix in something short.
- Don't set up a sentence just to end it on a dramatic reveal after a colon ("The result: chaos."). Say the thing plainly.
- Don't reach for three of everything. If a sentence groups three adjectives or three examples out of habit rather than because there are exactly three, cut to what's actually needed.

## 7. Don't give things agency they don't have

- Software doesn't "empower users" or "navigate challenges" — people do things, tools do things mechanically. Say what the person can now do, or what the tool actually does, not what it heroically enables.
- Write like a peer explaining something, not like a brochure or a textbook. If a sentence would sound at home in marketing copy or an academic abstract, rewrite it or cut it.

## 8. Use plain typing punctuation, not typeset characters

Smart quotes, a true ellipsis glyph, and an em dash character are marks AI writing tools default to. People typing fast don't produce them — they type straight quotes, three periods, and a double hyphen. Match the human default:

- Straight quotes, not curly ones: "like this," not “like this” — for both double and single quotes/apostrophes.
- Three typed periods (...), not the ellipsis character (…).
- A double hyphen (--), not an actual em dash (—) character, on the rare occasion a dash survives the guidance in section 6 to avoid them in the first place.
- For number ranges, spell out "to": 10 to 20, not 10-20 or 10–20. For date ranges, use an en dash: 2023–2024, not a hyphen (2023-2024). A hyphen is for compound words only (well-known, twenty-two).
- Avoid Title Case For Every Word In A Heading. Use sentence case instead: capitalize only the first word and proper nouns.

Exception: watch for word processors auto-converting straight quotes to curly ones inside code blocks, inline code, file paths, or commands — those must stay literal ASCII or they'll break when copied and run.

## Final pass

Before delivering the document, reread it once and ask:

- Does every heading and bullet list still earn its place, or did prose get chopped up out of habit?
- If I read this aloud to the person who asked for it, would any sentence make me wince?
- Could I delete the first sentence of any section without losing anything? (Often yes — it's throat-clearing.)
- Does the ending add something, or does it just repeat what came before?
- Is there a sentence complimenting the reader, the topic, or the request anywhere in here?
- Any em dashes standing in for a period or comma? Any sentence set up purely to land a colon-reveal?
- Does anything in here have three of something just because three felt right?
- Does any sentence give an inanimate thing human agency ("the platform empowers...")?
- Any curly quotes, an ellipsis character, an em dash character, hyphenated number ranges (should read "X to Y"), hyphenated date ranges (should be an en dash), or Title Case headers outside of code and commands?

## Example

**Before:**

> It's important to note that in today's fast-paced business environment, customer onboarding is not just a process — it's a critical journey that can make or break the customer relationship. Our team has leveraged a robust, holistic approach to reimagine this experience.

> **Key Findings:**
> - Onboarding time was reduced
> - Customer satisfaction improved
> - The team did a great job on this initiative

**After:**

> Onboarding time dropped from 14 days to 6, and post-onboarding satisfaction scores rose from 72 to 89. The redesign focused on cutting handoffs between support teams, which was the main source of delay.

Same information, a third of the length, nothing left for the reader to wade through.
